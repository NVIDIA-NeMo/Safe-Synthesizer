# Spectral Similarity

Spectral Similarity checks whether synthetic numeric columns repeat in cycles
of the same lengths as the training columns. Column-level metrics, such as
histograms, compare which values appear. This metric compares how the values
rise and fall over time, broken down by how long each rise and fall takes.

Three properties matter most:

- Periodicity is whether values rise and fall in a repeating cycle, and how long
  one cycle takes, called the period or cycle length. Hourly temperature with a
  daily pattern has a 24-step cycle, and daily sales with a weekly pattern have
  a 7-step cycle.
- Cycle strength is how much of a column's variation a cycle holds compared
  with the rest. A strong daily pattern with little noise holds most of the
  variation, and the same pattern buried in noise holds only a little.
- Smoothness is whether the rest of the variation comes from slow drifts or from
  fast changes between neighboring values.

All three come from the power spectrum, which splits a sequence's variation
into cycles of every length and measures the share each length holds. This
guide calls that breakdown the spectrum and groups cycle lengths into bands.
The metric compares training and synthetic spectra one group-column pair at a
time. A group is one sequence, such as one sensor, as set by
`data.group_training_examples_by`, and a pair is one numeric column within one
group.

the order differs. The right column shows what the metric compares, which is
the share of variation in each cycle-length band.

## Reading the score

The score runs from 0 to 10, and higher means the synthetic data spreads its
variation across cycle lengths more like the training data does. Differences
small enough to be explained by chance do not lower the score, so synthetic
data generated by the same process as the training data scores highly. When the
training data has a cycle, synthetic data without it scores low, and synthetic
data with a cycle an octave or more away scores near 0. If the training data is
structureless noise, with no cycle and no particular smoothness, synthetic data
that is also structureless noise matches it and scores high.

The score ignores level, scale, and a straight-line trend, because the metric
removes the trend and compares each band's share of the variation. It also
ignores when in its cycle a series peaks, so a daily pattern that peaks at noon
and one that peaks at midnight look the same.

With about 200 points per sequence, scores fall into rough tiers, taken from the
[example scenarios](#example-scenarios):

- About 8 to 10 means the synthetic data keeps the training data's cycles and
  smoothness, as with the same process (about 10) or pure noise against pure
  noise (about 9.7).
- About 5 to 8 means the synthetic data keeps the main cycle but loses or adds
  part of the variation around it, as with a lost second cycle (about 7.5) or
  outlier spikes (about 6.2).
- About 2 to 5 means the synthetic data loses a large part of the structure, as
  with a cycle buried in three times the noise (about 4.9) or a missing cycle
  (about 2.6).
- About 0 to 2 means the synthetic data has lost the cycle or has one of the
  wrong length, as with shuffled values (about 1.4) or a cycle half as long
  (about 1.0).
- Exactly 0 means the synthetic values are constant, follow a straight line, or
  have fewer than `min_points` finite values, while the training values vary.

Use these tiers as a guide. Whether a difference matters depends on the use
case. For example, reproducing a daily cycle may be critical for demand
forecasting or staffing, while a workload that uses only daily totals might not
depend on it. Compare scores across runs only when the columns, groups, and
`max_cycle_length` are the same, because changing them changes what the score
measures.

The score covers only how variation is spread across cycle lengths. A low score
means the synthetic data does not reproduce the training data's cycles or
smoothness. The values themselves, their distributions, and the relationships
between columns can still match well, as with the shuffled and rearranged
series in the figure above, which have exactly the training values. Read this
score alongside the Synthetic Quality Score, and weigh it by how much your use
case depends on repeating cycles.

### When the score is available

The score needs:

- At least one numeric column in both the training and synthetic data.
- At least one group in both datasets, when the data is grouped by
  `data.group_training_examples_by`.
- Training sequences with at least `min_points` finite values that vary beyond
  a constant or a straight line.

Training sequences that fail the last requirement have no spectrum to compare,
so the metric skips their pairs and the card lists them under Skipped
comparisons. Synthetic sequences with the same problems are still scored, at 0.
When no usable pair is left, the card reports no score, and its note says why.

## Configuration

Time-series evaluation runs by default whenever `time_series.is_timeseries` is
true. It computes the metric and adds it to the Time-Series Metrics panel in the
Synthetic Quality section of the HTML evaluation report. Set
`evaluation.time_series.enabled: false` to skip all time-series metrics.
Setting it to `true` without time-series mode is a configuration error.

```yaml
time_series:
  is_timeseries: true
  timestamp_column: time
evaluation:
  time_series:
    enabled: null  # null follows time_series.is_timeseries
    spectral:
      value_columns: null
      max_cycle_length: 128
      min_points: 16
      max_groups: 128
```

Values are ordered by `time_series.timestamp_column` and split into sequences by
`data.group_training_examples_by`.

- Leave `value_columns` as `null` to evaluate every column that both datasets
  share and that is inferred as numeric. Set an explicit list to evaluate only
  selected numeric columns.
- Raise `max_cycle_length` when longer cycles matter for your use case. It sets
  the longest cycle, in observations, that the score tells apart from shorter
  cycles. The last band is centered at `max_cycle_length`, and every longer
  cycle and slow drift counts in that band too. Set it to 168, for example, so
  that a weekly cycle in hourly data gets its own band, separate from 128-step
  cycles. To also tell a weekly cycle apart from slower cycles, set it above
  the weekly length, such as 336. Sequences shorter than `max_cycle_length`
  distinguish cycles up to their own length.
- Raise `min_points` to leave out sequences too short to show the cycles you
  care about. Training sequences with fewer finite values are skipped, and
  synthetic sequences with fewer score 0.
- Set `max_groups` to cap how many shared groups are evaluated. When more
  groups are shared, the metric evaluates a reproducible random sample of them,
  and the result reports the total, evaluated, and omitted shared-group counts.

## Limitations

- The metric measures cycle lengths in observations, so it assumes that
  observations are evenly spaced in time. Irregular timestamps or long gaps
  distort the cycle lengths it sees.
- Short sequences carry less evidence, so their scores bunch toward the middle.
  Synthetic data from the same process still scores highly at any length of at
  least `min_points`. Training sequences with fewer finite values are skipped,
  and synthetic sequences with fewer score 0. A sequence of 30 observations
  shows at most about one 24-step cycle, so a missing daily cycle in 30 hourly
  values still scores about 6.
- A sequence cannot show cycles longer than itself.
- Cycles of similar length need long sequences to tell apart. A 26-step cycle in
  place of a 24-step one scores about 9.7 at 200 points and about 6.9 at 1,000.
- Differences in smoothness without a cycle also score more gently in short
  sequences. A much less persistent process scores about 7.2 at 50 points and
  about 3 at 1,000. [Autocorrelation Similarity](autocorrelation-similarity.md)
  measures persistence directly and is a better guide to it in short sequences.
- The metric evaluates each numeric column on its own, so it cannot see whether
  cycles in different columns line up with each other.
- Binary and categorical columns need different measures and are not selected
  automatically.
- The metric does not establish causality or show whether the synthetic data
  memorizes training sequences.
- The score summarizes all cycle lengths, columns, and groups in one number, so
  it can't tell you about a particular cycle. Check the report charts for the
  columns and groups that matter to you.

## Calculation

For every usable group and numeric column, the metric computes the spectrum of
each sequence from all of its values:

1. Remove a straight-line trend fitted at the values' original positions and
   center the result. Missing and non-finite values stay as gaps in place and
   contribute zero.
2. Compute the periodogram \(P_k = |X_k|^2 / n_\text{finite}\), where \(X_k\) is
   the discrete Fourier transform of the \(n\) values at frequency \(k / n\), for
   \(k = 1, \ldots, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor\). Frequency \(k\) corresponds to a
   cycle length of \(n / k\) observations. Each frequency below \(n / 2\) also
   stands for its negative-frequency partner, so it counts twice. At even
   \(n\), the two-step cycle \(k = n / 2\) has no partner and counts once.
3. Group frequencies into bands centered every half octave in cycle length,
   from 2 observations up to the shorter sequence's length or
   `max_cycle_length`, whichever is smaller. That longest cycle is always the
   last center, even when it falls less than half an octave above the one
   before it. Each frequency's weight falls linearly to zero at the
   neighboring band centers, and cycles longer than the last center belong to
   the last band. The share of band \(b\) is the weighted power in that band
   divided by the total power, so the shares sum to 1. The training and
   synthetic sequences use the same bands even when their lengths differ.

The metric then compares the training shares \(T_b\) and synthetic shares
\(S_b\) in three steps:

1. Estimate how much each share varies by chance. Periodogram noise at each
   frequency is exponential, with a mean equal to the local noise level. The
   level \(N_k\) is the median of the periodogram over the frequency and its two
   neighbors on each side, divided by \(\ln 2\), the median of a unit
   exponential, and capped at \(P_k\). A median ignores a single peak, so a
   cycle does not raise the noise level around it. A steady cycle of power
   \(C_k = \max(P_k - N_k, 0)\) on that noise varies by
   \(N_k^2 + 2 N_k C_k\), much less than its own power, so a cycle the
   synthetic data misses is not mistaken for noise. The two-step frequency of
   an even-length sequence has one degree of freedom, half as many as the other
   frequencies, so it varies by \(2 N_k^2 + 4 N_k C_k\). The variance of each
   band share follows by the delta method, including the division by total
   power, and is divided by the share of values that are present. The noise
   allowance \(\sigma_b^2\) adds the training and synthetic variances.
2. Sum the squared gaps between the shares and subtract the squared gap that
   chance alone would produce, \(\sigma_b^2\) per band. What remains is the
   excess difference, floored at 0.
3. Compare the excess with the larger of the two shares in each band, floored
   at the noise, so losing variation in a band and inventing it are penalized
   alike, and bands that hold more variation count more:

\[
\text{pair similarity} = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\max\left(0, \sum_b \left[(T_b - S_b)^2 - \sigma_b^2\right]\right)}{\sum_b \max(T_b, S_b, \sigma_b)^2}}
\]

Subtracting the expected chance difference before taking the square root
removes most of the penalty from chance, so two sequences from the same process
lose few points, and pooling the bands before the square root keeps several
small chance gaps from adding up to a large penalty. The result is clipped to the range 0 to 1. The
final 0–10 score is ten times the mean pair similarity across group-column
pairs. Therefore, 10 means the shares agree within chance, and 0 means the
synthetic data puts its variation at entirely different cycle lengths from the
training data.

Pairs without a usable spectrum are skipped or score 0, as
[When the score is available](#when-the-score-is-available) describes.
